01 / Follow the control flow

Try first. Decompose when needed.

Question: When does the planner enter the process?

The ADaPT idea is to use execution feedback to decide whether a task needs to be broken into smaller parts.

Our fixed scenario is “put a clean mug on the desk.” Step through an attempt and watch the active task change.

What the coach adds: a concrete task, explicit depth, and a visible reason for each branch.

Source and teaching assumptions

ADaPT, Section 3 and Algorithm 1. The outcomes here are scripted illustrations. The paper uses an LLM completion heuristic. AND requires the subtasks; an OR search stops when an alternative succeeds. A depth guard can return failure.

02 / Inspect the current state

A finished batch can unlock more work.

Question: Can we stop when no graph change is needed?

A means requirements, B content, C styling, and D integration. Each arrow is a prerequisite. D waits for both B and C.

At the batch boundary, B and C can be completed while D is ready but not yet completed. The graph can remain exactly the same.

What the coach adds: a distinction between “this running batch is finished” and “all nodes are completed.”

03 / Put numbers on visible objects

Change one edge. Inspect two scores.

Question: What does dependency complexity count?

Count all direct connections at each node, including incoming and outgoing edges. Standard deviation measures how much those counts differ.

The extra A → D edge is redundant in this example: D already waits for A through B and C. The layer counts stay 1, 2, 1.

What the coach adds: the equation becomes a calculation on four visible nodes.

Source and teaching assumptions

Flow v2, Section 3. Dependency complexity uses connection-count standard deviation. Parallelism averages task counts per layer. The graph is illustrative, its layout is schematic, and these scores do not establish actual execution time.

04 / Interpret the evidence

Partial credit is different from passing everything.

Question: What is the percentage averaging?

Imagine a slide deck that compiles and includes the required content but has the wrong slide count. Two of three requirements pass.

A 66.7% mean requirement score does not mean this deck fully meets the task.

What the coach adds: the denominator and aggregation are visible before a result is interpreted.

Source and teaching assumptions

Flow v2, Appendix B.1 averages three binary requirements. The deck here is hypothetical; its changing scores are not reported experimental data or success probabilities.
